\title{\textbf{Multi-Task Unified Prompting for Vietnamese and English AMR Parsing:\\
A Systematic Study of Decomposition Granularity}}
\author{Anonymous}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR) parsing is challenging because it requires
simultaneously predicting semantic concepts, their relations, and variable
bindings that encode coreference. We propose \textbf{Multi-Task Unified Prompting
(MTUP)}, a structured supervised fine-tuning framework that decomposes this joint
prediction into explicit intermediate steps within a single forward pass of an
instruction-tuned language model. Using LoRA-adapted Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, we
systematically evaluate four decomposition variants (2--4 steps) on Vietnamese
and English Little Prince corpora. Our experiments reveal a clear
\emph{language-dependent} interaction: on Vietnamese, the two-step MTUP2 variant
achieves the highest SMATCH of 0.6494 (3-seed mean), outperforming both
end-to-end baselines; on English, direct generation remains optimal (B1:
0.6747), and no decomposition variant surpasses either baseline. We further
show that more intermediate steps do not monotonically improve performance---
error accumulation and over-specification at intermediate stages consistently
reduce final accuracy. Together, these findings provide the first systematic
empirical characterisation of decomposition granularity for AMR parsing and
establish a competitive neural baseline for Vietnamese, a previously
under-explored language in semantic parsing research.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Abstract Meaning Representation \citep{banarescu2013abstract} encodes sentence
meaning as a rooted, directed, acyclic graph in which nodes represent semantic
concepts and labelled edges represent semantic relations. AMR has proven
valuable across a range of downstream applications---machine translation,
question answering, summarisation, and information extraction---yet parsing
natural language into AMR remains a difficult structured prediction problem.
Three distinct sub-problems must be solved jointly: \emph{concept identification}
(grounding words in PropBank frames and ontological entries), \emph{relation
labelling} (assigning typed edges between concept nodes), and \emph{variable
binding} (assigning unique identifiers that encode reentrancy, i.e.\
within-sentence coreference). End-to-end sequence-to-sequence models conflate
all three, placing a high structural burden on a single decoding step.

The dominant paradigm for AMR parsing has shifted from hand-engineered
transition systems \citep{wang2015broad,damonte2017incremental} toward
sequence-to-graph neural architectures \citep{zhang2019amr,bevilacqua2021spring},
and more recently to large language models (LLMs) fine-tuned with structured
prompts \citep{xie2023universal,li2023gpt}. State-of-the-art systems achieve
SMATCH scores of 0.81--0.85 on AMR 2.0 \citep{li2023gpt}, yet the vast
majority of research targets English with large annotated corpora. The
effectiveness of prompt-based decomposition strategies for low-resource or
morphologically complex languages remains poorly understood.

In this work, we address this gap by proposing and systematically evaluating
\textbf{Multi-Task Unified Prompting (MTUP)}, a framework that decomposes the
AMR parsing objective into a sequence of intermediate generation steps, all
produced by a single fine-tuned model within one inference call. Rather than
engineering separate models or pipelines for each sub-task, MTUP encodes
multiple reasoning stages as structured markers in the assistant turn of a
chat-formatted training example. We evaluate four MTUP configurations (MTUP2
through MTUP4) and two end-to-end baselines on Vietnamese and English,
enabling the first controlled cross-linguistic comparison of decomposition
granularity in AMR parsing.

Our principal contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose MTUP, a unified supervised fine-tuning framework for
          multi-step AMR generation that requires no additional inference cost
          relative to end-to-end baselines.

    \item We provide the \textbf{first neural AMR parsing results for Vietnamese},
          establishing a quantitative baseline for a morphologically complex,
          low-resource language with no prior public benchmark.

    \item We demonstrate a \textbf{language-dependent decomposition effect}: MTUP2
          outperforms direct generation on Vietnamese (SMATCH 0.6494 vs.\
          0.6466 for the strongest baseline), while direct generation remains
          optimal on English---a cross-linguistic reversal that challenges
          universalist assumptions about decomposition.

    \item We show that decomposition performance is \textbf{non-monotonic in step
          count}: MTUP2 (two steps) outperforms MTUP3A, MTUP3B, and MTUP4
          (three or four steps) on Vietnamese, indicating that error propagation
          and intermediate over-specification outweigh the benefits of finer
          decomposition.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{AMR Parsing}

Early AMR parsers relied on transition-based algorithms \citep{wang2015broad}
and graph-based structured prediction with hand-engineered features
\citep{damonte2017incremental}. The field transitioned to neural
sequence-to-graph architectures with the work of \citet{zhang2019amr}, who
demonstrated competitive performance by treating AMR as a transduction task.
\citet{bevilacqua2021spring} introduced SPRING, a bidirectional Transformer
system that achieves 0.77--0.78 SMATCH on AMR 2.0 without any specialised
post-processing.

The emergence of LLMs has shifted the frontier further. \citet{xie2023universal}
showed that instruction-tuned T5 and BART models can match or exceed
task-specific systems through prompt-based fine-tuning. \citet{li2023gpt}
demonstrated that GPT-4 with few-shot chain-of-thought prompting achieves
0.81--0.82 SMATCH on AMR 2.0, approaching human inter-annotator agreement.

\subsection{Multi-Step and Chain-of-Thought Reasoning}

The idea of decomposing complex tasks into intermediate reasoning steps has
strong theoretical and empirical motivation. \citet{wei2022chain} showed that
chain-of-thought prompting substantially improves LLM performance on arithmetic
and symbolic reasoning. \citet{nye2021show} demonstrated that explicit
intermediate symbolic representations improve generalisation in neural networks.
For structured prediction specifically, \citet{jia2016semantic} proposed
intermediate meaning representations for slot-filling semantic parsing; more
recent work on code generation \citep{guo2020towards} has shown that
plan-then-generate strategies outperform single-pass generation on complex
synthesis tasks.

In the AMR domain, \citet{ulrich2023amr} explored in-context learning with
large models. Our work is distinguished by its focus on \emph{fine-tuned}
decomposition within a single model and its systematic comparison across
decomposition granularities, rather than the use of few-shot prompting with
fixed decompositions.

\subsection{Multilingual and Low-Resource Semantic Parsing}

Cross-lingual AMR has received limited attention.
\citet{damonte2018cross} studied cross-lingual transfer for AMR, relying on
machine translation as an intermediate step. Vietnamese NLP resources remain
sparse: foundational syntactic corpora were contributed by
\citet{nguyen2009vietnamese}, and \citet{vu2018vislp} provided shared task
benchmarks for POS tagging and dependency parsing. To our knowledge, no prior
work has reported SMATCH results for Vietnamese AMR parsing with neural methods.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}

LoRA \citep{hu2021lora} has become the dominant approach for adapting
7--13B-parameter models to new tasks within memory-constrained settings.
\citet{zhang2023qlora} demonstrated that 4-bit quantisation with LoRA can
match full fine-tuning on larger models, and \citet{wang2023lora} showed that
LoRA-fine-tuned 7B models are competitive with full fine-tuning of 13--70B
models on structured prediction tasks. Our choice of LoRA on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
reflects this established practice.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}

\subsubsection{Vietnamese AMR}

We use a Vietnamese AMR corpus derived from selected chapters of \textit{Le Petit
Prince} (``Hoàng Tử Bé''), annotated with guidelines adapted from the English
AMR standard to address Vietnamese-specific morphosyntactic phenomena: the
classifier system (người, cái, con, chiếc, etc.), negation encoding (\texttt{:polarity -}),
and modality (\texttt{:modality +/-} for \textit{được}/\textit{bị}).
The corpus was split into two complementary annotation batches
(train\textsubscript{1}: 1,235 sentences; train\textsubscript{2}: 572 sentences)
to allow weighted training; after deduplication by sentence, we obtain
approximately 2,951 unique training examples per method (train\textsubscript{1}
with weight 1, train\textsubscript{2} with weight 3). The held-out test set
consists of 149 sentences drawn from the same narrative domain.

\subsubsection{English AMR (Little Prince)}

For cross-lingual comparison, we use the English Little Prince AMR corpus, which
provides standard AMR annotations for the English source text. The corpus
contains 1,274 training sentences (single source, no duplication) and a
provided test split of 143 sentences. We apply the same LoRA configuration and
training procedure as for Vietnamese, using the English AMR system prompt and
\texttt{no\_var\_mode=clean} to handle reentrancy expansion.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol}

We report SMATCH F1 \citep{cai2013smatch}, the standard AMR evaluation metric
that computes precision and recall over matched concept-relation triples.
To account for training variance, all models are trained with three random
seeds (42, 123, 456), and we report the mean and standard deviation across
seeds. We additionally report: \emph{parsability} (fraction of outputs with
balanced parentheses), \emph{root accuracy} (fraction of correctly identified
root concepts), and \emph{average output length} (mean character count of
predicted AMR strings, used as a proxy for structural complexity).

Table~\ref{tab:vietnamese} presents Vietnamese results. MTUP2 achieves the
highest SMATCH score (0.6494), surpassing the stronger of the two baselines,
B2, by 0.28 percentage points in absolute terms. The gap to B1 (0.5917) is
substantially larger (+9.8\% relative), suggesting that the no-variable
intermediate representation provided by B2 and MTUP2 is genuinely beneficial
for Vietnamese. We note that the MTUP2 advantage over B2 is modest and within
one standard deviation; we therefore do not claim statistical significance, but
rather interpret MTUP2 as the most \emph{efficient} decomposition: it
introduces one intermediate representation with low additional error risk.

A clear non-monotonic pattern emerges across decomposition depth. MTUP2
(2 steps) outperforms MTUP3A (3 steps, +1.4pp), which in turn outperforms
MTUP4 (4 steps, -0.4pp relative to MTUP3A). MTUP3B (3 steps with explicit
relations) is the worst-performing MTUP variant (0.5891), performing
comparably to the weaker baseline B1. This ordering is consistent with the
error-accumulation hypothesis: each additional intermediate step introduces a
new locus of error that may cascade to the final output.

Table~\ref{tab:english} presents English results. In contrast to Vietnamese,

suggest a genuine interaction between decomposition strategy and language
properties.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Decomposition Helps Vietnamese but Not English}

We attribute the language-dependent pattern to two complementary factors.

\paragraph{Morphological and semantic complexity.}
Vietnamese relies on a rich classifier system and employs modality and
polarity markers that have no direct English counterparts. These features must
be encoded in AMR through language-specific conventions (e.g.,
\texttt{:classifier}, \texttt{:modality +/-}). The no-variable intermediate
representation in MTUP2 provides an explicit checkpoint at which the model
commits to graph structure before resolving variable identifiers, potentially
reducing the risk of conflating structurally distinct phenomena. English AMR, by
contrast, maps more directly from surface form to graph structure, offering
less benefit from an intermediate staging step.

\paragraph{Dataset size and scaffolding.}
The Vietnamese training set is larger in raw sentence count (2,951 after
deduplication) but covers a narrower morphosyntactic range than the full AMR
2.0 English corpus typically used in prior work. In small-data regimes,
intermediate representations can act as inductive biases that guide the model
toward well-formed outputs---a role analogous to auxiliary training objectives.
The English Little Prince corpus (1,274 sentences) appears sufficient for
direct end-to-end learning, making additional scaffolding unnecessary.

\subsection{Non-Monotonic Decomposition Performance}

A consistent finding across both languages is that decomposition performance
is non-monotonic in the number of intermediate steps. On Vietnamese, the
ranking MTUP2 $>$ MTUP3A $>$ MTUP4 holds, with MTUP3B (the variant
introducing explicit relation lists) as an outlier performing below even B1.
On English, the same monotonic trend holds: MTUP2 $>$ MTUP3A $>$ MTUP3B,
MTUP4 last.

We attribute this to two mechanisms. \emph{Error propagation}: each additional
step multiplies the opportunity for a generation error to corrupt all subsequent
steps. \emph{Over-specification}: forcing the model to explicitly enumerate
concepts or relations as flat lists constrains the generation space in ways
that may misalign with the model's internal representations, learned during
pre-training on natural language. The flat list format for concepts and
relations is a novel output form that requires additional learning, whereas
Penman AMR notation is well-represented in pre-training data.

\subsection{Significance of the MTUP2 Margin on Vietnamese}

The absolute advantage of MTUP2 over B2 on Vietnamese (0.6494 vs.\ 0.6466,
$\Delta$=+0.28pp) is small relative to the cross-seed standard deviation
($\sigma_{\text{MTUP2}}$=0.003, $\sigma_{\text{B2}}$=0.005). We therefore
refrain from strong claims of superiority and instead highlight MTUP2 as a
\emph{robust} choice: it achieves the highest observed mean without the
increased variance of longer decompositions (e.g., $\sigma_{\text{MTUP4}}$=0.013),
making it the most reliable option in this setting.

\subsection{Limitations}

This study has several important limitations that bound the scope of its
conclusions.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Domain restriction.} All experiments use a single narrative
          domain (Little Prince). Results may not generalise to news, scientific,
          or conversational text, which exhibit different vocabulary and semantic
          structure densities.

    \item \textbf{Training regime.} Vietnamese models are trained for 25 epochs
          without a development set for early stopping, which risks overfitting.
          English experiments use the provided development split; a comparable
          held-out set for Vietnamese would strengthen the evaluation.

    \item \textbf{Model and scale.} We evaluate a single model family
          (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) with LoRA. Whether the decomposition effects
          reported here generalise to other model sizes or families is an open
          question.

    \item \textbf{Absence of cross-lingual baselines.} We do not compare against
          multilingual transfer approaches (e.g., AMR annotation projection via
          translation), which represent an important alternative for low-resource
          settings.

    \item \textbf{Statistical testing.} The sample sizes (149 and 143 test
          sentences) and the number of seeds (3) limit the statistical power of
          cross-method comparisons. Differences of less than 1pp absolute should
          be treated as indicative rather than definitive.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practical Guidance}

For practitioners building AMR parsers for new low-resource languages, our
results suggest the following heuristics. If the target language exhibits
morphological complexity or requires many language-specific AMR conventions, a
two-step decomposition (structure without variables, then variable assignment)
is a robust starting point. For languages with simpler surface-to-AMR mappings
or larger annotated training sets, direct end-to-end generation may be
preferable. In both cases, our findings argue against speculative addition of
intermediate steps: the empirical evidence suggests diminishing---and
potentially negative---returns beyond two steps.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented MTUP, a multi-task unified prompting framework that
decomposes AMR parsing into structured intermediate generation steps within a
single fine-tuned language model. Systematic evaluation of four decomposition
variants across Vietnamese and English reveals two principal findings.

First, \emph{decomposition effectiveness is language-dependent}. MTUP2 is
the best-performing method on Vietnamese (SMATCH 0.6494) but not on English,
where direct end-to-end generation achieves the highest score (B1: 0.6747). The
cross-linguistic rank reversal of B1 and MTUP2 constitutes strong evidence
against universalist claims about decomposition-based prompting.

Second, \emph{more steps do not monotonically improve accuracy}. Across both
languages, MTUP2 outperforms all longer decompositions. Error propagation and
the learning cost of novel intermediate formats appear to outweigh the
cognitive-scaffolding benefits of finer decomposition.

Beyond these findings, this work establishes the first quantitative neural
benchmark for Vietnamese AMR parsing, filling a gap in the multilingual AMR
literature. Future work should extend the evaluation to typologically diverse
languages, larger training sets, and multiple model families to determine the
boundary conditions under which structured decomposition is beneficial.
